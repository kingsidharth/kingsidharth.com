A product requirements document describes what should happen. An eval decides
whether it did. Only one of those two survives contact with a model.

The shift is not that specs got replaced. It is that the spec became executable,
and the moment a spec is executable it stops being a document and starts being a
test suite with opinions.

## What changes when the spec runs

Write "the assistant should be concise" in a PRD and you have written nothing.
Six people will read six different sentences. Write it as an eval — median
response under 90 tokens on this fixture set, no regression on the helpfulness
rubric — and you have written a constraint that can fail.

The failure is the point. A requirement that cannot fail is not a requirement.

> The question is never "is the model good." It is "good at what, measured how,
> compared to what it did last Tuesday."

## Three things to write down before the first eval

- **The fixture set.** Twenty real inputs beat two thousand synthetic ones. Pull
  them from whatever your users actually typed.
- **The comparison.** Against the previous version, against a naive baseline, or
  against a human. Absolute scores are decoration.
- **The thing you refuse to trade.** Every eval suite optimises something at the
  expense of something else. Name the something else before you start.

Skip the third and you will ship a model that is measurably better and
noticeably worse.

## Where it breaks

Evals rot. The fixture set stops resembling production, the rubric drifts, and
one day you are optimising a number that no longer points at anything. Budget
for re-grounding the suite as seriously as you budget for writing it.

The PRD never had this problem, because the PRD never told you anything.
