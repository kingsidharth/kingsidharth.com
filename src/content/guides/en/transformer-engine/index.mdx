Every large language model — GPT, Claude, Llama, all of them — is the same
machine. Text goes in the bottom as numbers, something comes out the top as a
distribution over what comes next. Everything in between is one block, copied
and stacked.

This is the machine, exploded. It stays on screen; scroll, and it takes itself
apart for you.

      <section id="overview" data-engine-level="overview" className="flex min-h-[80svh] flex-col justify-center py-16">
        <h2>The machine, whole</h2>
        <p>
          Bottom to top: raw text becomes token ids, ids become vectors, vectors
          ride a conveyor belt through a stack of identical blocks, and at the
          top the last vector is read against every word the model knows. One
          distribution over what comes next. That's the whole trick.
        </p>
        <p>
          The phosphor dots climbing the middle are the data flow — the engine
          idling. Drag to orbit, scroll to travel downward into the parts, tap
          anything with a pulsing outline to dive inside it.
        </p>
      </section>

      <section id="tokens" data-engine-level="tokens" className="flex min-h-[90svh] flex-col justify-center py-16">
        <h2>Tokenisation</h2>
        <p>
          A neural network cannot read. Before anything else happens, raw text
          is cut into pieces and each piece is replaced by an integer from a
          fixed vocabulary.
        </p>
        <pre><code>"What is the capital of France?"  →  [3923, 318, 262, 3139, 286, 4881, 30]</code></pre>
        <p>
          Those seven integers are the entire input. Everything the model knows
          about your sentence, it reconstructs from them. Note <code> the</code> —
          the leading space is part of the token.
        </p>
        <h3>Tokens are not words</h3>
        <p>
          Modern tokenizers learn the vocabulary from data, so common strings
          become single tokens and rare ones shatter. <code>the</code> is one
          token; so is <code> the</code> with the leading space — a different
          id. <code>unbelievable</code> might be three. A rare emoji can cost
          one token per byte.
        </p>
        <p>
          This is why models fumble character-level tasks — counting the
          letters in "strawberry". They never see the letters. They see chunk
          ids, and the chunks don't align with the characters you see.
        </p>
        <p>
          Two consequences: context length is counted in tokens, not words —
          and token boundaries are invisible to you but load-bearing for the
          model.
        </p>
      </section>

      <section id="embeddings" data-engine-level="embeddings" className="flex min-h-[90svh] flex-col justify-center py-16">
        <h2>Embeddings</h2>
        <p>
          The embedding layer is a lookup table with one row per vocabulary
          entry and one column per dimension of the model's internal width —
          <code>d_model</code>, from 768 in small models to 16k+ in large ones.
          Token id 4881 — <code> France</code> — goes in, row 4881 comes out.
          The entire operation is indexing — and yet this table is learned, end
          to end, like everything else.
        </p>
        <p>
          After training, the geometry is not arbitrary: tokens used similarly
          land near each other. The table is where "France" first becomes
          something the machine can compute with. Not a definition of the
          word — a starting position the blocks above will rewrite beyond
          recognition.
        </p>
        <h3>Position is added, not inherent</h3>
        <p>
          A row of the table knows nothing about where its token sits. So a
          position signal is added to each vector — sinusoidal or learned
          embeddings in older models, rotary encodings (RoPE) applied inside
          attention in most modern ones. Each column of numbers now carries
          <em>what</em> the token is and <em>where</em> it sits.
        </p>
        <h3>The residual stream begins</h3>
        <p>
          These position-tagged vectors are the first state of a conveyor
          belt: one vector per position, flowing upward through every block.
          Each block reads the belt, computes something, and <em>adds</em> its
          contribution back — it never overwrites. That one choice is why a
          96-layer model can be trained at all: the blocks don't replace the
          signal, they annotate it.
        </p>
      </section>

      <section id="block" data-engine-level="block" className="flex min-h-[90svh] flex-col justify-center py-16">
        <h2>One block</h2>
        <p>
          A transformer is a stack of identical blocks — 12 in the original
          small ones, 96 in GPT-3, more since. Understand one block and you
          understand the architecture. One block does exactly two things, in
          order:
        </p>
        <pre><code>x = x + Attention(LayerNorm(x))     # positions talk to each other
x = x + MLP(LayerNorm(x))           # each position thinks alone</code></pre>
        <p>
          That <code>x +</code> in front of everything is the residual stream.
          Each sub-layer reads a normalised copy of the stream, computes, and
          adds its result back. "Add &amp; norm" in the diagram is exactly
          this: the add is the residual connection, the norm is LayerNorm
          keeping the numbers at a scale the next stage can digest.
        </p>
        <h3>The division of labour</h3>
        <p>
          <strong>Attention is the only place tokens mix.</strong> Everywhere
          else, each position's vector is processed independently. Attention
          is the one communication channel, moving information between
          positions weighted by relevance.
        </p>
        <p>
          <strong>The MLP is where positions process.</strong> After attention
          delivers context to a position, the MLP transforms that position's
          vector — same MLP, every position separately, no cross-talk.
          Attention gathers, the MLP digests. Once per block, dozens of times.
        </p>
        <p>
          Modern transformers are "pre-norm": LayerNorm acts on a copy inside
          each sub-layer while the stream flows through untouched except for
          the additions — a clean gradient highway from output back to
          embedding. And roughly two-thirds of a block's parameters live in
          the MLP, one-third in attention.
        </p>
      </section>

      <section id="attention" data-engine-level="attention" className="flex min-h-[90svh] flex-col justify-center py-16">
        <h2>Attention, opened</h2>
        <p>
          Attention is the mechanism that lets one token position read from
          the others. Every position's vector is projected three ways, by
          three learned matrices:
        </p>
        <ul>
          <li><strong>Query (Q)</strong> — what this position is looking for.</li>
          <li><strong>Key (K)</strong> — what this position advertises.</li>
          <li><strong>Value (V)</strong> — what it hands over if selected.</li>
        </ul>
        <p>
          Same input, three interpretations. A query asking "what is the
          subject of this sentence?" scores highly against keys whose tokens
          can be subjects.
        </p>
        <h3>The score grid</h3>
        <pre><code>scores = Q · Kᵀ / √d</code></pre>
        <p>
          One number per (reader, source) pair. Then the <strong>causal
          mask</strong>: no position may look at the future, which turns the
          grid into a triangle — the half-lit plates in the drawing. Softmax
          turns each surviving row into weights that sum to 1.
        </p>
        <pre><code>out = softmax(Q · Kᵀ / √d) · V</code></pre>
        <p>
          Each position's new state is a weighted average of every past
          position's value vector. Attention, stripped of mystique, is a
          content-addressed blur: "mix into me a little of everyone who
          matches what I'm looking for."
        </p>
        <h3>Why "multi-head"</h3>
        <p>
          The model runs several attention operations in parallel — the heads
          — each with its own smaller Q, K, V, each free to specialise: one
          tracks the current subject, another resolves pronouns, another
          matches brackets. Their outputs are concatenated and passed through
          one more matrix, <code>Wₒ</code>, back down to <code>d_model</code>.
          Probe a trained model and the heads are not random: previous-token
          heads, delimiter heads, "induction heads" that power in-context
          learning. None of it is programmed.
        </p>
      </section>

      <section id="mlp" data-engine-level="mlp" className="flex min-h-[90svh] flex-col justify-center py-16">
        <h2>The MLP</h2>
        <p>
          The MLP gets a single box in most diagrams and most of the
          parameters in the model. Inside, it is almost disappointingly
          simple:
        </p>
        <pre><code>h = GELU(x · W↑)       # widen: d_model → 4 × d_model
y = h · W↓             # narrow back: 4 × d_model → d_model</code></pre>
        <p>
          Two matrix multiplications with a nonlinearity between them. No
          cross-token communication — the same MLP is mapped over every
          position independently.
        </p>
        <p>
          The hidden layer is four times wider than the stream. In that
          expanded space the vector can be picked apart into many more
          features; GELU — a rounded ReLU — soft-gates the ones that don't
          fire, and the down-projection writes the surviving conclusions back
          onto the stream.
        </p>
        <h3>Where knowledge lives</h3>
        <p>
          Attention moves information already in the context. The MLP is where
          information from <em>training</em> gets applied. Interpretability
          work finds hidden neurons that fire on strikingly specific patterns
          — text in French, legal citations, numbers being compared. One crude
          but durable mental model: <strong>attention retrieves from the
          context, the MLP retrieves from the weights.</strong>
        </p>
      </section>

      <section id="output" data-engine-level="output" className="flex min-h-[90svh] flex-col justify-center py-16">
        <h2>The full stack</h2>
        <p>
          After the last block, three steps turn the stream into an output. A
          final LayerNorm steadies the scale. Then the <strong>unembedding</strong>:
          the last vector is dotted with every row of the vocabulary — usually
          the very same embedding table, used in reverse — producing one logit
          per entry. That's where the logits in the <a href="/guides/sampling/logits-and-softmax">sampling
          guide</a> come from; softmax makes them a distribution, and the
          sampler picks one token. <code>Paris</code>, say.
        </p>
        <h3>One token per revolution</h3>
        <p>
          That token is appended to the input and the <strong>entire machine
          runs again</strong>: embed, all the blocks, norm, unembed, sample.
          Generation is a loop, not a pass — a 500-token answer is 500 full
          revolutions of the engine.
        </p>
        <pre><code>prompt → [engine] → token₁
prompt + token₁ → [engine] → token₂
prompt + token₁ + token₂ → [engine] → token₃</code></pre>
        <p>
          The only mercy is the KV cache: keys and values for old positions
          don't change, so they're stored instead of recomputed — why the
          first token is slow and the rest stream out fast.
        </p>
        <p>
          Strip it down and a transformer is: a lookup table, one repeated
          block with a communication channel and a per-position function, the
          table read backwards, and a sampler. The engine is simple. What it
          learns to be is not.
        </p>
      </section>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>
