The MLP gets a single box in most diagrams and most of the parameters in the
model. Inside, it is almost disappointingly simple:

</div>

```
h = GELU(x · W↑)       # widen: d_model → 4 × d_model
y = h · W↓             # narrow back: 4 × d_model → d_model
```

Two matrix multiplications with a nonlinearity between them. No cross-token
communication — the same MLP is applied to every position's vector
independently, like a function mapped over a list.

## Why the widening matters

The hidden layer is four times wider than the stream. In that expanded space
the vector can be picked apart into many more features than `d_model`
dimensions could hold at once; GELU then acts as a soft gate, zeroing the
features that don't fire and passing the ones that do. The down-projection
writes the surviving conclusions back onto the stream.

GELU itself is a rounded ReLU: near-zero inputs leak through slightly instead
of being hard-clipped. The curve glyph in the diagram is the whole function.
Everything interesting is in the matrices around it.

## Where knowledge lives

Attention moves information that is already in the context. The MLP is where
information from *training* gets applied. Mechanistic-interpretability work
finds individual hidden-layer neurons that fire on strikingly specific
patterns — a neuron for text in French, a neuron for legal citations, a
neuron for numbers being compared — and the down-projection rows read like a
library of facts to add to the stream.

One crude but durable mental model: **attention retrieves from the context,
the MLP retrieves from the weights.** A block runs both lookups per pass, and
a stack runs the pair dozens of times.

## Why nobody diagrams it

Because there's nothing to draw. It has no grid of scores, no triangle mask,
no heads — just a wide room the vector passes through. But when a model
"knows" that Paris is in France, that fact was retrieved here, in a block's
MLP, as a pattern match against weights frozen at the end of training.
